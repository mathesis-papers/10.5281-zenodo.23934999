The §2.9 conjecture of Lester, Haddadin and Gripaios (arXiv:2008.05206v6): three parity-odd variables are necessary

Haobo Ma, Wenlin Zhang

Paper

Can two continuously varying numbers always detect whether a particle event differs from its mirror image, while ignoring reference-frame changes and exchanges of indistinguishable particles? Haobo Ma and Wenlin Zhang answer no for the event classes studied here: every such pair misses some genuinely handed event. Combined with earlier constructions by Lester, Haddadin and Gripaios, their result makes three numbers necessary and sufficient for two specified four-particle classes.

An event consists of four momenta, each recording energy and three spatial components. Energy must be nonnegative and at least the spatial momentum’s magnitude; masses may vary, and zero momenta are allowed. The particles form two separately interchangeable pairs. Allowed frame changes preserve spatial orientation and time direction; parity reverses every spatial momentum. An event is chiral, or handed, if no allowed frame change and pair exchanges reproduce its parity image. The collision subclass additionally imposes a strict, Lorentz-invariant nondegeneracy condition on the incoming pair. Neither domain requires the four momenta alone to conserve momentum.

The measurements must be continuous, unchanged by allowed symmetries, and sign-reversing under parity. They therefore vanish on achiral events. A set is sufficient if its measurements never all vanish on a chiral event; it detects handedness rather than reconstructing the event. Lester, Haddadin and Gripaios had supplied sufficient triples, called S for the full domain and S-hat for collisions. Their conjecture asked whether any alternative one- or two-number construction could work, not merely whether entries could be deleted from their triples. The authors’ universal lower bound excludes every such alternative.

The proof constructs a sphere’s worth of chiral collisions. Two fixed incoming momenta have different squared masses and span the time–longitudinal plane. Three sphere parameters control the outgoing pair’s energy difference, difference of squared transverse lengths, and opposite longitudinal components. All momenta remain strictly future-timelike. Suppose an allowed transformation produced a mirror image. Different incoming masses forbid exchanging that pair. Combining the transformation with parity then leaves an orientation-reversing orthogonal transformation on the transverse plane. It cannot fix both independent outgoing transverse directions. Exchanging the outgoing particles instead forces all three parameters to vanish, impossible for a point on the sphere. Thus the entire sphere stays chiral.

Opposite sphere points are also parity partners up to an allowed rotation and outgoing-pair exchange. Any two admissible measurements consequently give a continuous map from this sphere to the plane, with opposite points receiving opposite values. The Borsuk–Ulam theorem guarantees that some opposite points receive equal values. Values that are both equal and opposite must be zero, producing the required chiral event that both measurements miss. Pairing a single measurement with an identically zero second measurement excludes one-number solutions too. Lester’s earlier chiral-path argument obstructed one continuous scalar; the sphere forces two to fail simultaneously.

The authors extend the lower bound without establishing matching upper bounds throughout. Their sphere already admits an additional unobserved future-timelike momentum completing conservation. Appending any number of identical small-mass momenta preserves the argument: mass differences prevent permutations from mixing these additions with the original particles. This covers an interchangeable incoming pair and any interchangeable outgoing collection of at least two particles. Distinct mass ranges also preserve the obstruction when all four or more momenta are interchangeable. These constructions allow variable masses; they do not settle prescribed equal-mass or massless configurations.

Two contrasts expose the importance of the assumptions. Without particle exchanges, the determinant with four labeled momenta as columns is a sufficient continuous parity-odd scalar: it is nonzero exactly on chiral events. Dropping continuity instead permits selecting the first nonzero component of a sufficient triple, an earlier construction credited to Lester, Haddadin and Gripaios. This preserves invariance, sign reversal and the correct zero set, but cannot be continuous everywhere. A complex scalar offers no escape either: its real and imaginary parts are two real measurements and must vanish together somewhere chiral.

The manuscript supplies written proofs and reports a pinned Lean declaration for the main lower bound on the two original four-momentum domains, not for the sufficient triples or additional corollaries. Its script checks algebraic identities and samples 31 rational sphere points and added-particle families of total sizes four through twelve. Finite sampling does not prove the universal claims; those rely on the geometric and topological arguments. The specific advance is exact minimality for the original triples and broader lower bounds. Exact minima for five-momentum classes, fixed-mass variants, and independence of larger previously displayed sets remain unresolved here.
